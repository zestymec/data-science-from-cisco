1. Introduction

Data science is the practice of collecting, managing, analysing and communicating data to support better decisions. Companies such as Cisco employ teams of “data crunchers” (including interns) who turn raw data into insight. The course covers the fundamentals: how data is collected, stored and used, how it is engineered for analytics, and how it powers AI.

2. What Data Actually Is

Data is any recorded fact, measurement or observation. Every form we fill in, every post we publish and every behaviour we show online produces data. Data is created in three main ways:

Volunteered

Data people knowingly share: sign-up forms, surveys, profiles, posts.

Observed

Data captured by watching behaviour: clicks, location, purchases, device usage.

Inferred

Data derived from other data: predicted interests, credit scores, recommendations.

Data in daily life

We are more connected than ever. Everywhere we go and everything we do becomes a new source of data. On social media, our decisions, and even what we are about to buy, can be connected and analysed. Because of the sheer volume of data now available, organisations can track, isolate and predict behaviour at a scale that was impossible before.

Takeaway: know whether data is volunteered, observed or inferred. It affects accuracy, privacy and ethics.

3. Types of Data

Data is first split into quantitative (numbers that can be measured or counted) and qualitative (descriptions and categories such as colour, opinion or name). Quantitative data is then either discrete or continuous.

Discrete data

Collected by counting. Whole, concrete numbers, usually described as “the number of…”.

  • Customers who bought bicycles
  • Employees in each department
  • Litres of diesel purchased each week for a delivery truck

Continuous data

Collected by measuring. Values fluctuate and can take any value in a range.

  • Temperature inside the store
  • Speed of a rider in a race
  • Distance travelled by a moving bicycle

Programming data types

When data is stored or processed, each value has a technical type:

TypeMeaningExample
StringText"Karachi"
IntegerWhole number42
FloatDecimal number36.6
BooleanTrue or falsetrue
Date/timeA point in time2026-10-08
Takeaway: choosing the correct type decides which chart, statistic and model you can use.

4. Structured vs Unstructured Data

Structured

Organised in rows and columns with a fixed schema, e.g. spreadsheets and SQL tables. Easy to search and analyse. Stored in relational databases (MySQL, PostgreSQL).

Unstructured

No fixed format: emails, images, video, audio, social posts. Harder to analyse but very rich. Often stored in non-relational (NoSQL) databases (MongoDB, Cassandra) or data lakes.

Semi-structured data, such as JSON and XML, sits between the two: it has tags or keys but no rigid table.

5. Data Visualization

Purpose. Charts and graphs transform raw data into easy-to-understand formats that highlight trends and patterns. A table gives exact values; a graph shows the shape of the story.

Selection. Match the chart to the pattern you want to show. Not every chart fits every dataset.

Four factors when choosing a chart

Variables

How many characteristics are measured.

Data points

The volume of information within each variable.

Time

Whether the data tracks change over time (hourly, daily…).

Relationships

Whether you need to compare or correlate data points.

GoalBest chart
Change over timeLine chart
Compare categoriesBar / column chart
Parts of a wholePie or stacked bar (few categories only)
Relationship between two variablesScatter plot
Distribution of valuesHistogram or box plot

6. Big Data and the Four Vs

Big data’s characteristics change how data is collected, transmitted, stored and accessed. The four Vs describe those characteristics and the challenges they create for data infrastructure engineers.

Volume

The amount of data. Terabytes to zettabytes need scalable, distributed storage.

Velocity

The speed at which data is created and must be processed, sometimes in real time.

Variety

Many formats and sources: text, sensors, images, logs, transactions.

Veracity

The trustworthiness and uncertainty of data: errors, bias, missing values.

Takeaway: veracity is the one analysts forget. Bad data in means bad decisions out.

7. The Potential Benefits of Data Growth

More data, handled well, creates value in everyday life:

  • Healthcare: earlier disease detection and personalised treatment.
  • Retail: demand forecasting and personalised recommendations.
  • Transport: live traffic routing and predictive maintenance.
  • Agriculture and climate: better yields and early disaster warnings.
  • Public services: smarter planning and humanitarian response.

8. Data Pipelines

Managing big data means moving it reliably from where it is created to where it is used. A data pipeline has three phases:

1. IngestionCollect data from apps, sensors, databases and APIs, in batches or streams.
→
2. TransformationClean, filter, validate, reformat and combine data so it is analysis-ready.
→
3. StorageSave in a database, data warehouse or data lake for analysts and models.
Takeaway: data scientists often spend most of their time on the transformation (cleaning) phase.

10. Analytics for Business and Humanity

Business understanding

Analysis starts with a clear business question. Data analytics for business insights helps with customer behaviour, sales trends, cost reduction and risk.

Humanitarian insights

The same techniques support aid and development: tracking disease outbreaks, mapping disaster damage and directing relief where it is needed most.

11. Pivot Tables and Charts in Excel

A pivot table summarises large tables by grouping and aggregating (sum, count, average) without writing formulas. A pivot chart visualises that summary.

12. AI and Machine Learning

Artificial intelligence (AI)

The broad field of making machines perform tasks that normally need human intelligence: reasoning, language, perception.

Machine learning (ML)

A subset of AI where systems learn patterns from data instead of following hand-written rules.

AI: fact and fiction

  • Fact: AI is already used in search, translation, fraud detection and recommendations.
  • Fact: AI quality depends on the quality and quantity of its training data.
  • Fiction: AI “thinks” or understands like a human. Current systems find statistical patterns.
  • Fiction: AI is always objective. It can inherit bias from its data.

13. Conclusion

Data science is a chain: data is created, classified, collected through pipelines, stored, analysed, visualised and finally used to train AI. Each link depends on the one before it, so quality data and clear questions matter more than any single tool.
